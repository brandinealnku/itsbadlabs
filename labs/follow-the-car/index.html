Documented 2026 case: an incorrect stolen-plate database entry contributed to Flock alerts and a police stop of an innocent driver.

In Plymouth, Minnesota, in June 2026, Flock cameras twice flagged a vehicle after a stolen-plate record had been entered incorrectly into a national database. The driver was confronted by multiple police vehicles and patted down before officers determined the database entry was wrong. Evidence behind the experience

What Flock says its LPR data can contain

Flock's current LPR policy says LPR data can include plate and vehicle images, plate number and state, vehicle characteristics such as color and make, date, time, and camera location. It says queries are audit-logged and LPR data is hard-deleted on a rolling seven-day basis by default, subject to customer law or policy. Read Flock's policy ↗

Why civil-liberties critics remain concerned

The ACLU argues that networked ALPR systems can create location-surveillance risks because repeated observations and data sharing can expose patterns of movement at scale. Read the ACLU analysis ↗

Documented alert consequence

The Plymouth sequence shown in the experience is based on a 2026 CBS Minnesota report involving an incorrect stolen-plate database entry and subsequent Flock alerts. Read the report ↗

What is simulated here

The vehicle, locations, timing, routine inference, searchable profile, and on-screen interface are fictional and designed to illustrate how records can accumulate. They are not presented as a reconstruction of a specific person's movements.

ITSBAD Labs · Follow the Car · interactive public-literacy experiment. The active experience separates observation, inference, and documented consequence so the simulation does not present inference as confirmed fact.
